\begin{abstract}
Radio-frequency machine-learning (RFML) experiments often rely on separate tools for waveform synthesis, channel modeling, dataset preparation, model training, and field-data analysis. Integrating them for each new study increases setup effort and makes consistent signal and channel metadata difficult to preserve. This paper presents a flexible, research-enabling toolbox that unifies parameterized waveform generation, channel augmentation, dataset management, neural-network training, inference, and visualization. Its channel layer supports configurable impairments, stochastic models, and reusable interfaces for Sionna-compatible digital twins and measured channel responses, while its data layer accepts recorded in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) data. Dataset-wide augmentation draws per-example parameters from a reproducible run-level seed, and each example retains its waveform, channel, and processing metadata. The principal contribution is an open-source, publicly accessible workflow that enables researchers to move from a signal or propagation hypothesis to repeatable training and inference without developing a new data pipeline for each study. At the workshop, a two-stage live demonstration will allow participants to explore this workflow, including ray-tracing examples using the bundled digital twins of the University of Texas at Austin (UT Austin) and Hat Creek Radio Observatory (HCRO).

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Spectrum-sharing systems depend on timely and trustworthy observations of the radio environment. Automatic modulation classification (AMC) can contribute to this situational awareness by assigning received samples to candidate signal families without complete transmitter-side information. Reported classification performance, however, can overstate field performance when the training and test sets share the same signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), channel distribution, or collection geometry. A model trained only on idealized waveforms may learn features that are unstable under location-dependent multipath or receiver impairments \cite{oshea2018}.

Conducting such studies commonly requires researchers to connect waveform generators, propagation simulators, measurement files, learning frameworks, and visualization utilities through experiment-specific scripts. This fragmentation slows exploratory work and creates opportunities for inconsistent preprocessing, sample-rate assumptions, data partitions, and metadata. A reusable end-to-end environment can reduce this integration burden while making clean, simulated, and measured conditions easier to compare under a common experimental configuration.

This work presents an interactive radio-frequency machine-learning (RFML) toolbox for that purpose. The software links waveform generation, channel augmentation, visualization, dataset construction, model training, inference, and evaluation through a shared desktop workflow. Each example remains associated with the waveform parameters, channel configuration, source asset, and processing decisions used to produce it. Researchers can therefore change a waveform, channel source, digital twin, or classification model without reconstructing the remainder of the experiment. The toolbox is intended to support rapid hypothesis testing while keeping the metadata needed for repeatable comparisons and later field validation.

The work is motivated by the National Science Foundation (NSF) Spectrum Innovation Initiative: National Radio Dynamic Zones (SII-NRDZ) program. A national radio dynamic zone (NRDZ) is a spatial region, potentially spanning noncontiguous frequencies, in which automated spectrum-management mechanisms regulate electromagnetic energy entering, leaving, or occupying the zone \cite{nsfnrdz}. The MITRE-led SPARKIE effort is developing an experimental dynamic-spectrum-sharing environment at the Hat Creek Radio Observatory (HCRO), where radio astronomy observations must be protected from harmful interference while permitting controlled spectrum use \cite{mitrenrdz}. In this work, we use our toolkit to develop a classifier and accompanying pipeline to identify this interference. While the toolkit is application agnostic, we use this NRDZ at HCRO as an example for the rest of the paper. This includes a ray-traced digital twin of the NRDZ region as well as on-site field recordings captured with software-defined radios and measured channel responses. All of which entered the same workflow through the interfaces described in Section~\ref{sec:architecture}.

The paper's primary contribution is an open-source, flexible, and easy-to-use toolbox for rapid and repeatable RFML experimentation. First, its unified architecture connects MATLAB-based waveform synthesis, configurable impairments, stochastic and deterministic channel modeling, dataset management, PyTorch-based training, inference, and visualization. Second, it provides interchangeable input paths for user-supplied Sionna/Mitsuba digital twins, complex channel responses stored in MATLAB or NumPy files, and recorded in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) data represented with the Signal Metadata Format (SigMF), with all inputs handled through a unified data-management workflow. Third, a live demonstration at the workshop will show how the toolbox operates in practice, allowing participants to move through the end-to-end workflow from waveform synthesis and channel augmentation to small-scale training and single-waveform inference. The bundled University of Texas at Austin (UT Austin) and HCRO scenes, together with channel estimates from the channel-measurement-augmented training (CMAT) pipeline \cite{cmat}, will serve as examples that users can exchange and extend.


\section{Background, Related Work, and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Deep neural networks can learn modulation-dependent structure directly from time-domain I/Q samples, but their performance depends on the propagation and acquisition conditions represented during training. In field deployments, received signals vary with transmitter--receiver geometry, multipath, fading, noise, carrier-frequency offset, and receiver effects. Prior over-the-air studies have shown that these differences can materially affect classification performance \cite{oshea2018}. A model trained only on idealized waveforms or a narrow set of channel conditions may therefore perform poorly at a new location or under previously unseen propagation conditions. Robust RFML development requires training data that represents both general channel variation and the site-specific conditions relevant to deployment.

Three complementary channel-augmentation approaches can introduce this variation without collecting and labeling every waveform class under every condition. Stochastic models efficiently generate many statistically varied channel realizations. Ray tracing (RT) computes channels from three-dimensional scene geometry, material properties, antenna configurations, and transmitter--receiver locations. And channel banks acquired from real-world measurements can be reused and applied new datasets. All three approaches can augment synthetic datasets, and ray-traced and measured channels further allow researchers to investigate particular locations using either a digital twin or propagation observed at the site. Together, these approaches broaden the channel conditions represented during training and reduce dependence on large site-specific waveform collections, although their accuracy still depends on model fidelity, scene quality, measurement calibration, and supporting metadata.

Existing open-source systems address many discrete parts of this workflow. Sionna provides GPU-accelerated link-level simulation and native neural-network integration, while Sionna RT extends it with differentiable geometry-based propagation modeling \cite{sionna,sionnart}. TorchSig provides RFML dataset generation, signal transforms, and domain-specific augmentation in PyTorch \cite{torchsig}, and HermesPy combines link-level simulation with software-defined-radio verification through a Python interface \cite{hermespy}. The presented toolbox fills the remaining integration gap by placing configurable impairments and the three channel-augmentation approaches behind a common user-facing interface. It connects Python- and MATLAB-based waveform generation, site-specific digital twins, measured-response banks, recorded I/Q data, dataset organization, model training, and single-example inference through a unified workflow. Fig.~\ref{fig} summarizes these data paths.

\section{Toolbox Workflow}
\label{sec:architecture}

The toolbox is implemented as a PySide6 desktop application organized around the experiment lifecycle rather than a single propagation model, waveform family, or classifier. Separate views support waveform generation, channel augmentation, dataset visualization, model training, and inference. A shared dataset manager transfers samples and metadata between these views so that a user can proceed step by step through a complete experiment without assembling separate conversion and orchestration scripts. MATLAB supplies most waveform-generation routines. NumPy and SciPy support signal processing, PyTorch supports model training, and Sionna RT provides the ray-tracing backend. The figures in this section illustrate the implemented workflow through a simple classifier trained on simulated waveforms augmented with ray-traced channels from the HCRO digital twin and then applied to data collected onsite at HCRO.
\subsection{Waveform Synthesis and Inspection}

The waveform generator accepts the sampling frequency $f_s$, carrier frequency $f_c$, symbol period $T_s$, modulation order $M$, number of symbols, pulse shape, roll-off factor, and output representation. Thirteen selectable families are implemented: pulse-amplitude modulation (PAM), quadrature-amplitude modulation (QAM), phase-shift keying (PSK), frequency-shift keying (FSK), frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS), linear frequency modulation (LFM), Barker-coded pulses, frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW), Wi-Fi, Long Term Evolution (LTE), fifth-generation New Radio (5G~NR), IEEE~802.15.4 (Zigbee), and long range (LoRa) chirp spread spectrum. PAM, QAM, and PSK also return the underlying symbols for constellation inspection. The interface displays time-domain, frequency domain, and constellation views before a sample is stored.

All thirteen selections are reached through a single dispatcher, which calls MATLAB through the MATLAB Engine for Python for every family except LoRa, whose chirps are computed directly. The dispatcher returns exactly $N=N_{\mathrm{sym}}\operatorname{round}(f_sT_s)$ samples. PAM, QAM, and PSK use the selected root-raised-cosine (RRC) or rectangular pulse shape. The remaining families bypass that filter and use the constructions in Table~\ref{tab:waveforms}. Standard-based waveforms are resampled to $f_s$ where required and then fitted to $N$. A frame shorter than $N$ is repeated, and a longer one is clipped at a randomly chosen offset so that repeated calls do not return the same deterministic preamble. Each family can be returned as complex baseband or converted to a real passband signal by quadrature upconversion at $f_c$. Complex baseband is the default, since both the stochastic and ray-traced channel paths require it.

The linear-modulation functions use Communications Toolbox and Signal Processing Toolbox primitives. LFM, Barker, and FMCW use Phased Array System Toolbox; Wi-Fi, LTE, and 5G~NR use WLAN, LTE, and 5G Toolbox, respectively; and Zigbee uses the Communications Toolbox \texttt{lrwpan} package. LoRa is generated in closed form and requires no toolbox, so it remains available when MATLAB is absent. Both real passband and complex baseband outputs are available. The stochastic and ray-traced channel paths require complex baseband I/Q input; converting a real passband array to a complex data type is not considered an equivalent representation. This constraint is enforced by the channel interface.

\subsection{Channel Augmentation}

The channel-augmentation view provides four interchangeable options that cover common needs while allowing researchers to incorporate their own channel data. The first applies additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN), scalar amplitude and phase changes, and carrier-frequency offset. The second applies a stochastic tapped-delay-line channel with a configurable profile, delay spread, SNR, and random seed. The third computes channel taps with Sionna RT using a user-selected scene, transmitter and receiver positions, antenna arrays, center frequency, sampling rate, and propagation-solver parameters. The fourth applies a selected or randomly drawn channel response from an imported bank, allowing measured, precomputed, or otherwise user-supplied channels to enter the same workflow. Any option can be applied to a single waveform for inspection or across an entire dataset folder. During a dataset augmentation run, each example receives its own parameters and, where applicable, its own channel response from a generator initialized with one run-level seed, providing variation while preserving reproducibility.


For the static multipath channels used in the current implementation,
augmentation is performed by discrete-time convolution between each transmitted
stream and its corresponding channel impulse response, followed by additive
receiver noise. For a multiple-input multiple-output channel, the received
sample at antenna $r$ is
\begin{equation}
y_r[n] =
\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tx}}}
\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}
h_{r,t}[\ell]x_t[n-\ell] + w_r[n],
\label{eq:mimo-channel}
\end{equation}
where $h_{r,t}[\ell]$ is the $L$-tap channel impulse response from transmit
antenna $t$ to receive antenna $r$, and $w_r[n]$ is additive receiver noise.
The channel taps remain fixed over one generated example.
Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}(a) shows the Sionna RT interface implemented in the toolbox. Transmitter and receiver positions may be entered as coordinates or selected by clicking the rendered geometry. In the latter case, the interface ray-casts onto the scene surface. The experiment configuration records the Sionna version, maximum interaction depth, enabled propagation mechanisms, ray sample count, antenna pattern, array dimensions, and tap interval.


\subsection{Digital-Twin Scene Import}
\label{sec:scenes}

The ray-tracing view accepts a Sionna/Mitsuba Extensible Markup Language (XML) scene together with its referenced mesh and texture assets. A scene may therefore be built in Blender or another compatible modeling workflow and loaded without changing the channel or learning components. Transmitter and receiver positions, antenna arrays, carrier frequency, and solver settings remain experiment parameters rather than properties fixed by the imported scene.

The toolbox distribution includes two ready-to-run digital twins, one for the UT Austin campus and one for HCRO. Both scenes incorporate site features derived from OpenStreetMap (OSM) data
(\textcopyright{} OpenStreetMap contributors, available under the Open Database
License) \cite{osm} and use Sionna radio materials based on International
Telecommunication Union Radiocommunication Sector (ITU-R) recommendations
\cite{itup2040}. The UT Austin scene covers approximately $0.8$~km by $0.6$~km and uses a ground plane to provide an urban campus environment for configuring transmitter--receiver locations and inspecting simulated paths. The HCRO scene covers approximately $4$~km by $2$~km and supplements the shared map-derived geometry with custom ground shaping created in Blender to represent the rugged terrain and vegetation observed onsite. For either scene, the toolbox computes complex path coefficients, delays, and angles for the transmitter--receiver geometry selected by the user. Users may also load their own Sionna/Mitsuba scenes without changing the downstream channel or learning workflow. Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}(a) shows the HCRO digital twin loaded in the toolbox's ray-tracing view.

\subsection{External Channel and Recording Import}
\label{sec:channel-import}

The external-channel interface accepts a single file or a directory of user-supplied channel assets. MATLAB inputs may contain a one-dimensional complex impulse response or frequency response. NumPy inputs may use the same representation or an $N\times2$ array whose columns store I and Q. The import configuration identifies the array variable, response domain, sample rate for an impulse response, frequency spacing or occupied bandwidth for a frequency response, center frequency when applicable, channel identifier, and normalization or calibration convention. Accepted arrays are converted to complex single precision, aligned with the waveform grid, and stored with their import metadata.

The fourth option allows users to import pre-extracted channel responses and apply them to generated waveforms through a common interface. The CMAT collection used in the demonstration is one example of this workflow \cite{cmat}. Its channel responses were estimated from measurements collected at HCRO, allowing synthetic waveforms to be convolved with propagation observed at the site rather than with a simulated model. The toolbox aligns the selected response with the waveform, performs the discrete convolution, adds receiver noise at the requested SNR, and records the channel source and processing configuration in the output metadata. Implementation and channel-estimation details are available in the CMAT repository \cite{cmatrepo}.

Recorded I/Q data may also be imported as a SigMF Recording comprising a \texttt{.sigmf-data} file and its \texttt{.sigmf-meta} description \cite{sigmf}. A supported complex datatype and \texttt{core:sample\_rate} are required; capture frequency, class labels, and valid sample regions are read from capture and annotation metadata when available. Imported regions retain their original length and are padded only when required by the selected model. SigMF recordings are treated as observed I/Q rather than as channel responses, unless a channel estimate has been derived separately.


\subsection{Dataset and Learning Components}

Generated and augmented signals are stored as NumPy arrays with JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) metadata. The metadata record includes the signal class and generation parameters. Augmented examples additionally record the selected augmentation and its configuration. The learning view discovers labeled examples, forms training and validation partitions, and provides four PyTorch architectures. The standard one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1-D CNN) contains 16- and 32-filter convolutional stages, while the compact 1-D CNN contains a single 8-filter stage, both use max pooling and adaptive average pooling before classification. The multilayer perceptron (MLP) contains hidden layers of 256 and 128 units and provides a feedforward baseline. The one-dimensional residual network (1-D ResNet) uses an initial convolution followed by six two-convolution residual blocks at widths 64, 128, and 256 and global average pooling. It adapts residual skip connections to one-dimensional signal sequences rather than reproducing the published ResNet configuration \cite{he2016}.

Samples are padded or truncated to a fixed length before training, and each example is power-normalized so that absolute scale does not distinguish classes. A saved model carries metadata in its class labels, signal length, hyperparameters, and source directories. The training view reports learning curves for a completed small-scale run (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}(b)). The inference view loads a saved model, accepts a freshly generated waveform or one recording imported from disk, reports the predicted class and confidence, and displays the selected example in time and frequency. Fig.~\ref{fig:inference} shows this path applied to one onsite HCRO recording.

\section{Live Demonstration}
\label{sec:demo}

The proposed live demonstration will show how a participant uses the toolbox from end to end. It is organized as a two-stage walkthrough (interactive waveform generation, channel augmentation, and small-scale training, followed by single-waveform inference) and is intended to make the integrated workflow concrete. The individual steps described below are illustrated in Figs.~\ref{fig:workflow} and~\ref{fig:inference}; the demonstration performs them in sequence, live, on the exhibition floor.

\textit{Stage 1---Interactive generation, augmentation, and training:} A participant selects a waveform family and one of four channel paths: AWGN and configurable impairments, a stochastic channel, ray tracing, or a pre-extracted CMAT channel response. For ray tracing, the participant may also choose a scene and radio node placement. The toolbox generates a small labeled batch, applies the selected augmentation, and displays clean and augmented examples. A compact model is then trained on this small dataset so that the configuration and learning curves can be inspected during the session. Prepared checkpoints remain available if runtime prevents the training run from completing live.

\textit{Stage 2---Single-waveform inference:} The trained or prepared model is applied to one generated or imported waveform. Where venue rules and hardware setup permit, an Ettus Universal Software Radio Peripheral (USRP) X410 supplies a live recording. Otherwise, cable transmission or prerecorded waveforms serve as a fallback. The interface displays the selected waveform together with its predicted class and confidence. The emphasis is the ability to move through the workflow and exchange its inputs, not the accuracy of one prediction.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations bound the present implementation. First, the standards-inspired waveform generators represent selected Wi-Fi, LTE, and 5G~NR configurations rather than exhaustive implementations of those standards. Second, ray-tracing accuracy depends on scene geometry, radio-material properties, antenna models, and solver settings. Third, a static channel response does not reproduce mobility, long-term time variation, oscillator drift, nonlinear front-end effects, cochannel interference, or a complete receiver link budget. External responses also require appropriate domain, sampling-rate or frequency-grid, and calibration metadata.

Near-term work will focus on further implementing and systematically testing these capabilities while making the toolbox more flexible across waveform sources, channel representations, scene configurations, and learning tasks. This effort will include improving metadata handling, validating simulated channels against site measurements, broadening support for imported data and channel models, and refining the interfaces through which new components are incorporated into the workflow.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a flexible, user-facing RFML toolbox that connects parameterized waveform synthesis, channel augmentation, dataset management, model training, inference, and visualization. Its primary contribution is an open-source, end-to-end workflow in which researchers can exchange digital twins, stochastic models, measured channel responses, recorded I/Q data, and learning architectures without rebuilding the surrounding pipeline. The bundled UT Austin and HCRO scenes and the imported CMAT responses illustrate this flexibility without making any one site or dataset a dependency. The HCRO-related examples (modivated by SII-NRDZ) exercise three input paths: a ray-traced digital twin, on-site estimated channel responses, and real-world captured I/Q data. The live demonstration shows how a participant can select a channel path and move from waveform generation through a small training run to single-waveform inference. By reducing the integration work between signal, channel, learning, and analysis components while retaining experiment metadata, the toolbox is intended to support faster, more repeatable RFML studies for dynamic-spectrum and spectrum-coexistence research. The software is released at \url{https://github.com/yliyli/RFML-Toolkit}.
